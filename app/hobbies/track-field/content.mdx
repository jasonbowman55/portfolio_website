## Introduction

I began track and field during my 7th grade year. I have competed in track ever since, all the way through my college career, where I competed for the Claremont McKenna track and field program as an engineering student at [Harvey Mudd College](https://www.hmc.edu/).

## Why Sports

I began competing in sports at a very young age, starting with T-ball as soon as my parents could register me. I went on to play baseball for nine seasons (T-ball through 8th grade), American football for seven (5th grade through 12th grade), and track & field for a decade (7th grade through my senior year of college). Through these experiences, I not only gained a deep understanding of each sport but, more importantly, learned valuable lessons about myself and how sports facilitated those lessons.

> A note on generalization: I'm not making a generalized or all-encompassing statement about sports and what they teach. While I believe these lessons and ways of thinking are common and apply to the majority of people involved in sports, I don't claim they're a one-size-fits-all experience.

The freedom to make mistakes under pressure, accept failure, analyze its cause, find a solution, and refocus — all within a low-stakes environment — was one of the most valuable lessons I gained from sports, particularly in my early years playing baseball and football. The key to growth under pressure is experiencing that pressure in the first place. If my athletic experiences hadn't provided this, I would have missed a crucial opportunity to develop resilience.

It's this combination of competing in a healthy way, working toward positive outcomes, and embracing challenges that made sports worth it from a young age. Baseball games, especially when I was just starting out, felt overwhelming and deeply significant in the moment — people were watching, coaches were instructing, and every action reflected the effort I'd put in. Football was the same: my team was counting on me to do my job, and coaches had put their trust in me. Learning to manage that stress and perform despite it shaped the way I handle challenges today, both on and off the field.

## Why Track?

Track and field is my most recent and current athletic endeavor, and the one I've stuck with the longest — 7th grade through the last meet of my college career. That last meet carried a bittersweetness I never felt ending football or baseball. I cried after my last football game but was ultimately happy to be done. Baseball was fun, but as I'll get into, I'm glad I got out when I did.

### Why Not Football?

To answer "why track," I think I first have to answer "why not football" — otherwise there'd be some lingering wondering if football could have been it. After 7 years, I know it wasn't for me. It kept me active, I met lifelong friends, and I built relationships with coaches and with myself that are invaluable — I wouldn't trade that time for anything. But even as a relatively decorated player by middle-and-high-school terms, I was never one to study the game, watch film, or practice on my own the way my teammates did. When I was excited to play, it wasn't for the game itself or the chance to win — it was the simple fact that I got to hit someone, clean and hard and fair, over and over again. That wasn't anger; it was an exercise in competitive drive. Over time I came to enjoy the one-on-one competitiveness and the feeling of going to war alongside your brothers. But that was it — football was my medium to exercise, to assert and re-assert myself, and to fight alongside a trusting group of teammates. It was never about the game for me, and that's why I say no to football.

### Why Not Baseball?

A simpler question. Once high school baseball arrived, something felt different — maybe I just enjoyed track more by then, and hindsight is 20/20. But I remember a different energy from staff and players when I tried to do both baseball and track at once. A level of politics that should never exist below the professional level started to show: parents got involved, coaches had favorites, teammates fought over playing time. Looking back, I'm confident I made the right call dropping baseball for track at that point.

## What Track Offers

### Individual Accountability

Your performance is entirely yours. No one else to blame, no team dynamics to navigate. You train, you compete, you own the results.

### Pure Meritocracy

The weight goes a certain distance. The clock shows a certain time. No politics, no favoritism — just measurable results.

### Weight Room Synergy

Track and field, especially the throws, directly supports and is supported by my love for weightlifting. The two reinforce each other in a way few sports can match.

### Community Through Competition

Paradoxically, track and field builds some of the strongest team bonds despite being an individual sport. Everyone is chasing their own goals, but we all cheer for each other — because someone else's success doesn't diminish ours.

## The People and Experiences

The relationships forged through track and field — teammates, coaches, competitors — have been among the most meaningful of my life. The sport has taken me across the country, introduced me to people from all walks of life, and taught me that true competition elevates everyone involved.
